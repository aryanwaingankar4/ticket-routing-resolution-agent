# Introduction

## Introduction {#s_intro}

Every medium or large organisation runs an IT service desk. Employees report problems such as a laptop that will not join the VPN, a slow database query or a shared folder they can no longer open, and each report becomes a *ticket* with a short title, a free-text description and, once closed, a resolution note. Behind the desk sit specialist teams for networks, databases, security, storage and so on. The desk's first job is **triage**: deciding which team owns the problem and placing the ticket in that team's queue.

Triage creates much of the delay in IT support. Users describe a *symptom* ("nothing loads since this morning"), not a *cause* ("the DNS server is down"), and one symptom can belong to several teams. A ticket in the wrong queue waits until someone there reads it, decides it is not theirs and passes it on, and every transfer adds another queue wait. {fig:lifecycle} shows this loop: a confident but wrong routing decision costs more than one that admits uncertainty and asks a person, because the error is discovered only after the wait.

[[FIG lifecycle | reports/black_book/fig/f_ticket_lifecycle.png | 4.8 | The life of a support ticket. A confident but wrong routing decision sends the ticket into a reassignment loop, and each transfer adds a queue wait before any work starts.]]

Automating triage with machine learning has been studied for many years [@shao], and modern language models can also draft a suggested fix. Most published work, however, reports only the share of tickets routed correctly. That hides the practical question: *what does the system do with the tickets it is likely to get wrong?* A classifier that is 75% accurate and silently guesses on the rest recreates the reassignment loop; the same classifier, if it hands a large part of its mistakes to a human, is far more useful. Drafted resolutions raise the same concern more sharply. A large language model (LLM) writes fluent, confident text whether or not it has a basis for it. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [@lewis] grounds the model in similar past cases, but only helps when the retrieved cases are relevant.

This project therefore treats **escalation to a human as a first-class output**. Our pipeline has two decision points, or *gates*. The first decides whether a fast classifier is confident enough to assign the team alone or whether a slower, stronger classifier must be consulted. The second decides whether the retrieved past tickets are similar enough for a language model to draft a resolution at all; if not, the ticket goes to a person and no language model is called. Each gate is a single threshold, and the central question is how to choose those thresholds from evidence rather than intuition. The work began as our final-year B.Tech project and grew into a research study written up as an IEEE-style paper; results that did not turn out as expected are reported as carefully as the positive ones.

## Problem statement {#s_problem}

The project is based on **Use Case 1 of the nasscom hackathon brief**, which asks for an AI-assisted agent that routes IT support tickets and suggests resolutions. The brief fixed:

- **Seven support categories**, treated as seven teams: Access Management, Application, Database, Infrastructure, Network, Security and Storage.
- **Routing**, **resolution suggestion** based on similar past tickets, **confidence-based escalation** to a human, and **automation flagging** of recurring issues.
- **Data:** a synthetic dataset generated with a language model, about 1,000 tickets with the fields *title*, *description*, *category*, *resolution* and *priority*.

{tab:brief} maps each element of the brief to a component of our system.

[[TAB brief | Elements of the nasscom Use Case 1 brief and the components that address them | 1.7,2.6,1.6]]
| Element of the brief | Component in this project | Described in |
| Seven categories | Fixed label set used by every classifier and dataset | {sec:s_dataset} |
| Routing | Two-tier classification cascade (TF-IDF, then BGE) | {sec:s_cascade} |
| Resolution suggestion | FAISS retrieval of five similar tickets and grounded drafting with Gemini | {sec:s_retrieval} |
| Confidence-based escalation | Cascade confidence gate (0.50) and retrieval similarity gate (0.67) | {sec:s_cascade}, {sec:s_retrieval} |

Stated as one problem: **given the text of a new IT ticket, assign it to one of seven teams and, when it is safe, draft a resolution grounded in similar past tickets; whenever either step is likely to be wrong, escalate the ticket to a human instead of guessing.** Mature tools exist for each component, so the difficulty lies in deciding *when* the system should act and showing with data that those decisions are sound. The brief asks for confidence-based escalation without saying how confidence should be measured or where the cut-off belongs, and it recommends synthetic data, which limits what any accuracy measured on it can mean. We therefore treated the corpus itself as an object of study (Sections {secn:s_dataset} and {secn:s_res_summary}).

## Scope of research {#s_scope}

The scope was set by the brief, by one academic year, and by our hardware, an ordinary laptop without a graphics card.

#### In scope

- English tickets (title and description) routed to the seven teams of the brief, through a complete pipeline: classification, retrieval, escalation, grounded drafting and offline automation flagging, exposed as an HTTP service and a demonstration interface.
- Calibration of both thresholds from measured data, including rejected attempts, and evaluation on out-of-template benchmarks, an adversarial escalation set, 108 tickets written by two team members and real user tickets from a public dataset.
- Comparisons that put the design in context (gate ablations, zero-shot LLM baselines, an audited LLM judge, conformal prediction and drift monitoring), and engineering practices that make every number reproducible.

#### Out of scope

- Live deployment: no real ticket stream has passed through the system and no engineer has acted on its output.
- Languages other than English, attachments and conversation threads; predicting priority or assigning individual engineers; training or fine-tuning large language models; formal user studies.

## Research hypothesis {#s_hypothesis}

The project was framed around five testable hypotheses, each tied to one of the gates or to the data used to fit and calibrate them ({tab:hypotheses}). Chapter 5 reports the outcomes, and {tab:hyp_outcomes} summarises the verdicts.

[[TAB hypotheses | Research hypotheses and how each one is tested | 0.45,2.2,1.75,1.5 | 9]]
| No. | Hypothesis | Measurable prediction | Tested in |
| H1 | One threshold on the top-1 retrieval similarity, chosen from in-domain and out-of-domain calibration tickets, decides escalation better than no gate | More adversarial tickets decided correctly with the gate than without | Gate ablation; similarity sweep ({sec:s_res_gates}) |
| H2 | Letting the fast lexical classifier answer above a calibrated confidence reduces latency without reducing accuracy | Lower median latency; accuracy indistinguishable from Tier 2 alone | Exact McNemar; latency ({sec:s_res_cascade}) |
| H3 | Coverage calibrated on paraphrased training tickets transfers to out-of-template text for embeddings but not for TF-IDF; the calibration distribution matters more than the method | Gap at α = 0.10 inside the noise band for BGE, outside it for TF-IDF | Split conformal prediction ({sec:s_res_conformal}) |
| H4 | A classifier trained only on templates keeps its accuracy on human-written and real tickets | Accuracy close to the benchmark | 108 author-written and 583 real tickets ({sec:s_res_independent}, {sec:s_res_real}) |
| H5 | Drafts above the gate are grounded, and an LLM judge can replace the human check | Most drafts grounded; judge agrees beyond chance | 33 human-labelled drafts; Cohen's kappa ({sec:s_res_ground}) |

The hypotheses were written so that a negative outcome would be informative. H4 was expected to fail in some form; the questions were how badly, and whether the gates would catch the resulting errors.

## Objectives {#s_objectives}

1. Build a synthetic corpus that follows the nasscom brief and measure what it can and cannot support.
2. Develop a two-tier cascade (TF-IDF backed by BGE embeddings) with a confidence threshold chosen from calibration data.
3. Build retrieval over past tickets with FAISS and a Gemini drafting step allowed only above a calibrated similarity threshold.
4. Evaluate both gates on out-of-template benchmarks and an adversarial set, with ablations that isolate each gate.
5. Compare the pipeline with zero-shot language models and audit an LLM judge of draft quality.
6. Study conformal prediction and drift monitoring as principled alternatives or additions to the fixed thresholds.
7. Test the unchanged pipeline on tickets no language model wrote: an independent, cross-labelled set and real user tickets.
8. Package the system as a tested HTTP service, a demonstration interface and a container image, with every number reproducible from committed files.
