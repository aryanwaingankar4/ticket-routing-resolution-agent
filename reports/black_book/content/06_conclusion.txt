# Conclusion and Future Scope

## Conclusion {#s_conclusion}

A triage system is valuable only if it knows when not to answer. Starting from Use Case 1 of the nasscom brief, we built a pipeline that routes IT tickets to seven teams and treats escalation to a human as a normal output, controlled by two separately calibrated gates. A confidence threshold of 0.50 decides when a fast TF-IDF classifier may answer alone, and a similarity threshold of 0.67 decides when Gemini may draft a resolution grounded in retrieved past tickets. Both values were chosen from measured calibration data, and the rejected attempts are recorded. The system is delivered as a FastAPI service, a Streamlit demonstration and a self-building Docker image, guarded by 456 automated tests.

The retrieval gate decides all nine adversarial tickets correctly, against three of nine without it, at the cost of letting 13.3% of out-of-domain calibration tickets through. The cascade does not improve accuracy over the embedding classifier alone (32/45 against 33/45, p = 1.000) but reduces median latency by 8% to 18%. A zero-shot language model classifies the benchmark more accurately (40/45), but it cannot abstain, which is exactly what our gates provide. A human judged 31 of 33 drafts grounded, while an LLM judge reached a kappa of −0.042 against that human, so automated judging could not replace human review.

The extended evaluation was the most informative part of the project. On 108 tickets written and blind cross-labelled by two team members (kappa 0.936), the cascade routed 74 of 98 correctly, similar to the benchmark, but the gate escalated 32 actionable tickets. On 583 distinct real tickets covering two teams, accuracy fell to 19.7% (50.4% under an alternative mapping), and 371 of 468 misrouted tickets still passed the gate. Across coverage guarantees, drift monitoring and classification, the same lesson recurred: the data a component is trained or calibrated on limits it more than the method chosen. These conclusions rest on a synthetic corpus, small evaluation sets, 108 author-written tickets and real tickets for two teams only, and should not be read as performance claims for a production help desk. We found no public, real, English IT ticket dataset whose categories match our seven teams; the Zenodo corpus was the closest real option, and our hand-written set covers all seven.

## Future Scope {#s_future}

- **Deployment and recalibration on real traffic.** Running the system in a live help desk would provide real tickets to recalibrate both gates, a deployment-traffic reference for drift monitoring, and a direct measure of misrouting cost and resolution time.
- **Training on real or more varied tickets.** The real-world result shows that template-generated training data is the binding constraint; real labelled tickets, or synthetic data written in many registers, are the most direct way to improve transfer.
- **A gate that sees routing errors.** Neither gate stops a misrouted ticket from reaching the drafter. Combining classifier confidence with agreement between the predicted team and the teams of the retrieved tickets is a natural next experiment.
- **Larger human-labelled sets.** Labelling more drafts, including direct labels for context sufficiency, would narrow the uncertainty on draft quality and allow any automated judge or second gate to be audited before use; labelling the 30 real-ticket drafts is the first step.
- **Broader real-world coverage.** A real benchmark covering all seven teams, and multilingual tickets, would test whether the recurring finding holds outside our corpus.
