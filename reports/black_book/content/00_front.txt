@@ABSTRACT
When an employee raises an IT support ticket, a triage system must send it to the team that owns the problem and, just as importantly, recognise the tickets it should not handle on its own. This project builds and evaluates such a system for seven support teams, starting from Use Case 1 of the nasscom hackathon brief. The pipeline treats escalation to a human as a normal output. A fast TF-IDF classifier answers when its confidence is at least 0.50; otherwise a classifier over BGE sentence embeddings decides. The five most similar resolved tickets are then retrieved, and Gemini may draft a resolution only if the closest one has a cosine similarity of at least 0.67; below that the ticket goes to a person and no language model is called. Both thresholds were chosen from measured calibration data, and the report records the calibration attempts that were rejected.

On a 9-ticket adversarial set the retrieval gate decides every ticket correctly, against 3 of 9 without it. The cascade is not more accurate than the embedding classifier alone (32/45 against 33/45, p = 1.000), but it cuts median per-ticket latency by 8% to 18%. A zero-shot language model classifies the 45-ticket benchmark better (40/45) but offers no way to abstain. A human judged 31 of 33 drafts grounded in the retrieved tickets, while an LLM judge reached a Cohen's kappa of −0.042 against that human. On 108 tickets written and cross-labelled by two team members (kappa = 0.936) the cascade routes 74 of 98 correctly, but the gate also escalates 32 actionable tickets. On 583 distinct real tickets covering two teams, accuracy falls to 19.7% (50.4% under an alternative category mapping), and 371 of 468 misrouted tickets still pass the gate. The recurring lesson is that the data a component is trained or calibrated on limits it more than the method chosen.

The system is delivered as a FastAPI service, a Streamlit demonstration and a Docker image, with 456 automated tests.
